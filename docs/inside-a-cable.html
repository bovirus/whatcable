Inside the Thunderbolt 4 example

Passive reference wiring shown in Thunderbolt 4 operation: eight coaxial high-speed conductors, USB 2.0 D+/D−, CC, two sideband conductors, VBUS and ground. Each plug contains an e-marker, local VCONN supply and Ra termination, plus a 10 nF VBUS bypass capacitor. The opposite plug follows the connection table below. Thunderbolt 4 connections operate at up to 40 Gbps. Compatible passive cables can also carry faster Thunderbolt 5 signalling between compatible endpoints; the TB4 label is not necessarily the cable’s physical speed ceiling.

The blue pairs, purple sideband paths and identity chip are additions to this particular basic USB 2.0 example. E-markers are also used in other USB-C cables. Shielding and other construction details can also differ.

A cable’s label is not always its physical limit

Thunderbolt 4 operation is limited to 40 Gbps. Compatible passive cables originally qualified for 40 Gbps can also carry Thunderbolt 5’s faster PAM-3 signalling. Both endpoints must support the newer mode; a Thunderbolt 4 port remains limited to 40 Gbps. Active cables contain signal electronics, so this should not be assumed for every active TB4 cable.

An older 40 Gbps e-marker identity alongside an 80 Gbps negotiated link is therefore not, by itself, evidence of misreporting. Intel explains compatibility with existing passive cables in its Thunderbolt 5 technical brief.

A cable is one part of the connection

WhatCable reads the information your Mac exposes about the port, cable, connected device or charger, and negotiated connection. For displays, it also examines the monitor and DisplayPort link. It compares those reports and uses available hub and Thunderbolt topology to distinguish the link to your dock from devices further down the chain.

For example: an 80 Gbps port connected to a Thunderbolt 4 device is limited to 40 Gbps by that device, even if the passive cable can carry faster signalling. Replacing only the cable will not make that device run faster. WhatCable’s diagnostic accounts for that rather than blaming the cable.

For displays: WhatCable Pro’s Display Diagnostics compares the current resolution and refresh rate, the monitor’s reported modes and the DisplayPort link. It accounts for Display Stream Compression (DSC) and reported adapters, helping explain why a display is running at a particular mode and what to check next.

Shielding

Conductive foil or braid helps reduce unwanted electrical interference. The outer braid encloses the cable conductors and connects to the metal shell in both plugs. Shell and ground are bonded inside each plug. The high-speed coaxial conductors have their own shields, selectable separately.

Power · VBUS

These conductors carry electrical power from the power source to the receiving device. USB Power Delivery lets compatible devices negotiate power over the CC connection. Charging depends on the cable, charger and device together. One red path represents a conductor group, not an exact wire count.

Sideband · SBU1 / SBU2

These two paths support mode-specific auxiliary signalling. Their role depends on the connection mode. For example, DisplayPort Alt Mode uses them for its AUX channel. They are not the main high-speed data pairs.

How WhatCable helps: WhatCable Pro’s Display Diagnostics examines the display link and reported adapter information alongside the monitor’s current mode. This helps explain a screen running below its expected resolution or refresh rate, including connections through HDMI conversion. It reads the reported link state; it does not directly test the individual SBU wires.

DisplayPort can train to a lower link rate for the mode currently selected. A lower rate alone does not establish a cable fault: selecting a higher resolution or refresh rate may cause the link to retrain. Adapter details are included when macOS exposes them.

E-marker · cable identity

A chip inside the plug tells connected devices what the cable declares it can support. It communicates over CC and receives power through VCONN. This reference has one e-marker in each plug. Its electrical connections are CC, local VCONN and ground, with Ra termination on VCONN. Full-featured cables require electronic marking, as do USB 2.0 cables above 3 A. Identity electronics do not make the data paths active. The block shows the chip’s function; package dimensions and PCB routing are not a manufacturer layout.

How WhatCable helps: WhatCable compares the e-marker’s declared capabilities with the Thunderbolt controller’s reported connection when available. Read those figures together: an older 40 Gbps identity can coexist with a valid 80 Gbps connection when a compatible passive cable carries newer signalling. That difference alone does not establish misreporting. A slower connection may instead reflect the port or device. Pro’s Negotiation Diagnostics shows the per-party figures behind the verdict.

A missing vendor ID does not by itself indicate a faulty or counterfeit cable. WhatCable considers it alongside the other identity information. Its trust checks distinguish informational notes from malformed or contradictory capability fields.

Coaxial shields

Each of the eight high-speed signal conductors has its own surrounding conductive shield in this coaxial construction. The shields connect to ground in both plugs. This is the coaxial option in USB-C Table 3-10; separate drain wires are not used. Shielded twisted-pair construction is another option and requires drain connections.

VCONN · chip supply

Contact B5 supplies the electronics inside this plug. It is separate from the VBUS conductors that charge your device. This reference uses an e-marker at each end, as in Figure 4-42. Each chip connects to local B5, CC and ground. No VCONN conductor crosses the cable. A local Ra termination from B5 to ground signals that cable power is needed. The small blocks show electrical functions rather than a specific chip package.

VBUS bypass capacitor

A 10 nF capacitor connects between VBUS and ground inside each full-featured plug. Table 3-10 specifies a minimum 30 V rating and placement close to the power pad. This reference uses Standard Power Range, not the higher-voltage Extended Power Range. The capacitor is not in series with the charging wires.

Thunderbolt 5: what changes

A short passive, EPR-capable reference: four high-speed differential pairs, USB 2.0, CC, sideband, power and ground. The same signal-path count as our Thunderbolt 4 example supports faster PAM-3 signalling. Each plug has an e-marker and local VCONN supply; the power path and bypass capacitor are rated for Extended Power Range. This illustrates a construction choice, not a teardown of a particular product.

Highspeed

Four differential pairs carry Thunderbolt 5 traffic using three-level PAM-3 signalling. The extra speed does not require extra pairs. Normal operation assigns two lanes in each direction for 80 Gbps each way. Bandwidth Boost assigns three lanes towards the display-heavy side and one back, giving 120/40 Gbps. Contact labels in the connection table identify physical wiring, not a fixed operating direction. These are link bandwidths; file-transfer throughput also depends on protocol overhead and devices.

Intel · Thunderbolt 5 signalling and bandwidth

Power

This EPR-capable example can carry up to 240 W: 48 V at 5 A, when the charger and receiving device negotiate it. Extended Power Range requires suitable cable insulation, connector construction and voltage-rated components, as well as an EPR-capable e-marker. The current is still 5 A; the higher voltage raises the power ceiling. Thunderbolt data speed and charging power are separate capabilities. A 240 W cable does not force a Mac to draw 240 W. Thunderbolt 4 cables can also support EPR; 100 W is the rating chosen for our TB4 reference, not a universal TB4 limit.

UL Solutions · 240 W EPR cable requirements

Marker

This reference retains one e-marker in each plug, powered locally through VCONN. Its identity includes the cable’s supported signalling and EPR capability. An EPR-capable 5 A cable permits charging contracts up to 48 V / 5 A (240 W), when both connected devices support them. The e-marker identifies capabilities; it does not itself generate the faster data signalling.

UL Solutions · EPR cable marking

Capacitor

A VBUS-to-ground bypass capacitor sits inside each plug, with a voltage rating suitable for EPR. This reference uses a 10 nF bypass capacitor rated at least 63 V for the EPR power path. The older 30 V minimum described for our SPR example is not suitable here. It connects across VBUS and ground, not in series with charging current. Its illustrated size is not a component footprint.

How to read this reference model

The electrical connections follow USB-C Release 2.0: §3.4 and Tables 3-10 / 3-11 define the cable wiring; §4.9 and Figure 4-42 describe the two-e-marker arrangement. Coaxial construction and that e-marker arrangement are permitted choices, not the only ways to build a cable. Colours, spacing and component blocks support inspection and are not manufacturing dimensions. Thunderbolt performance also requires its own compliance testing.

Thunderbolt 5 signalling follows Intel’s technology brief; its EPR power example uses the higher voltage and marking requirements described by UL Solutions. This does not mean every Thunderbolt 4 cable is certified for Thunderbolt 5. Each component’s expanded details link to the relevant specification section.
